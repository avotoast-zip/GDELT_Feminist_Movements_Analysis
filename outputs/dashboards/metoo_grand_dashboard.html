Update Summary (Week of Aug 3):

  • Searched the full article text for keywords, not just the web address and headline rematch tab Keywords found per article went from 1.02 to 1.53. Hashtag share rose from 52.8% to 54.7%. Backlash vocabulary like "false accusations," "witch hunt," and "due process" was almost invisible before, since that language mostly appears in the body text, not the headline. This also corrects an earlier report: the two vocabulary groups were said to overlap by 0.2%. On full text the overlap is 2.7%, and will rise further.
  • Removed duplicate articles using the article text fetch tab The first pass (web address + headline) removed 7,429. This pass compares the text itself and removes 33,940 more, leaving 76,925 unique articles. iHeartMedia's radio-station network alone produced 2,598 copies of 40 stories. The United States' share of the corpus dropped from 24.2% to 19.3% once syndication was removed.
  • Added a second dashboard page for fetching and deduplication fetch tab Covers what happened to every link, download success by country and year, how the corpus shape changed, and which sites produced the most duplicate copies.
  • Built an event timeline and measured how coverage spread events tab The Deneuve letter (France) reached 25 countries and most peaked the same day. The Kulturprofilen exposé (Sweden) reached 2 countries. #EleNão (Brazil) reached 1. The Weinstein story itself can't be measured, since it broke 10 days before the #MeToo hashtag existed, so keyword-based collection can't see its first 10 days.
  • Ran LDA topic modelling, one model per language topics tab Seven languages had enough articles: English, Spanish, French, Portuguese, German, Swedish, and Italian. Six were modelled in this run. Four data problems were found and fixed first: broken character encoding, website furniture (cookie notices, ad copy), junk form pages, and 7,485 stale pages that served today's content instead of the original article.

Still open

What we're actually studying. A sample of 398 downloaded articles found 17% of general-vocabulary articles are actually about the #MeToo movement, against 90% of hashtag articles. That implies roughly 42,000 of the 50,700 general-vocabulary articles are ordinary crime and court reporting, not movement coverage.
Labelling the full corpus. Needs an estimated $100–150 in AI credits. The project ran out before this could be finished.
Outlet names are collapsed. g1.globo.com, oglobo.globo.com and extra.globo.com all appear as globo.com. Outlet-level breakdowns understate how many distinct publishers are really in the corpus.
Stale pages. About 7% of downloaded pages served current site content instead of the original article at fetch time; 7,485 were caught and excluded from topic modelling.

Searching the full article text

Before this, keyword matching used only the web address and headline. 80% of articles have no headline saved, so most matches came from the web address alone: one or two words. An article holds hundreds. This pass searches the full downloaded text of 107,514 articles.

1.02 → 1.53keywords found per article
52.8% → 54.7%hashtag share of corpus (+2,004 articles gained a hashtag)
2.9% → 5.3%articles containing backlash vocabulary
71 → 2,899articles containing both support and backlash vocabulary
What it means. The overall change is small, and that matters: it means the split between hashtag articles and general-vocabulary articles isn't caused by weak searching. France is still 77% general vocabulary after reading the whole article. The two groups are genuinely different kinds of coverage, not an artefact of where the keywords were searched.
This corrects something reported earlier. The two vocabulary groups were said to be effectively separate, at 0.2% overlap. On full text the overlap is 2.7%, and will rise further as labelling continues. The earlier backlash figures by country were built on only the half of the vocabulary that headlines and web addresses could show.

Removing duplicates with article text

The first pass (web address + headline) removed 7,429 copies. This second pass compares the text itself: each article is cut into overlapping five-word chunks, and two articles are compared by how many chunks they share.

Overlap requiredDuplicate copies foundUnique left
100%, identical text24,16586,700
90% or more30,25980,606
80% or more (used)33,94076,925
Verified. On 99 sampled pairs, the true word overlap had a median of 100%. Zero pairs fell below 50%. These are literally identical texts, not near-misses.
One limitation. The rule that keeps a wire story separate when a newspaper adds its own editorial comment couldn't run properly. It needs stance labels we don't have yet. Only 130 articles were split out on text difference alone. Re-run after labelling.

Reading these numbers

Small-n peaks are noise. With 6 articles, the peak day is wherever 2 of them happened to land. Only countries with 15+ articles are used in the lag statistics.
A late peak may be a different event. Kavanaugh is the clearest case. The hearing was 27 September, confirmation was 6 October (day +9). Check what else happened that week before calling something a lag.
Download success varied by country, from 26% to 80%. A country with poor retrieval looks quieter than it was.
Absence here means absence in our corpus, not that the country ignored the event.

LDA topic modelling

LDA finds groups of words that appear together, called a topic, and scores each article on how much of each topic it contains. It doesn't name topics; that's done by hand afterward. Running all languages together just maps languages, not themes, so each language gets its own model.

Languages modelled. English (38,122 articles available, modelled on a sample of 25,000), Spanish (18,675), French (15,023), Portuguese (3,354), German (2,734), Swedish (2,079). 80,923 articles fed into these six models. Italian (936) also had enough articles by the same threshold but is not yet modelled in this run.
Four data problems fixed before this run. Broken character encoding in 4,314 articles (Spanish "prisión" stored as "prisiÃ³n," corrupting whole topics). Website furniture: a Swedish topic built entirely from "annons" (advertisement), a German one from the privacy policy, a French one from cookie notices. Junk pages, like an English topic that turned out to be a country dropdown from a registration form. And 7,485 stale pages serving today's content instead of the article, excluded.
Highlights: The Swedish Academy appears as its own isolated topic. The propagation analysis independently showed that story reached just 2 countries, two unrelated methods agreeing Sweden's wave was domestic. Separately, Spanish and Portuguese topics are dominated by individual femicide cases and court vocabulary, while English and French topics are dominated by the movement and named figures. This is the same hashtag-vs-general-vocabulary split found elsewhere in this project, appearing again without anyone labelling anything.
